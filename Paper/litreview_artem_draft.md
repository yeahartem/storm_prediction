## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 Background and Motivation

Physics-based simulation of subsurface engineering processes has reached a high degree of maturity, yet computational expense remains a persistent barrier to practical engineering use. In the domain of hydraulically fractured oil and gas wells, the post-fracturing **flowback** stage — during which the well is prepared for long-term production through cleanup of the hydraulic fracture — presents a representative example of this challenge. The process involves tightly coupled fluid mechanics and geomechanics phenomena, including multiphase displacement of non-Newtonian fracturing fluid, proppant pack compaction, grain embedment into fracture walls, and fines migration, all operating across length scales spanning several orders of magnitude (Osiptsov et al., 2020; Boronin et al., 2023). A comprehensive mechanistic model describing these phenomena has been developed and calibrated against field data from four multistage-fractured horizontal wells in Western Siberia (Vainshtein et al., 2020, 2021; Boronin et al., 2023), demonstrating that accurate description of well behaviour during flowback is achievable with a single physically interpretable tuning parameter.

Despite this accuracy, each simulation of the mechanistic model requires tens of minutes of computation per scenario. This computational cost renders the model impractical for tasks that require large ensembles of forward evaluations — in particular, sensitivity analysis, design space exploration, and inverse calibration of model parameters against field observations. A surrogate model capable of reproducing the mechanistic model's output at orders-of-magnitude lower cost is therefore a prerequisite for any practical inverse workflow. The full development and validation of such a surrogate for the fracture flowback problem, including a non-dimensional parameterisation that reduces the input space from over fifty physical variables to eight governing dimensionless groups, is presented in Galliamov et al. (2026); details of the physical model and dataset construction are given therein and in Boronin et al. (2023). The present paper builds directly on that foundation, extending the surrogate-only forward model into a coupled forward–inverse pipeline.

An analogous computational bottleneck arises in other domains with physically parameterised simulators: numerical weather prediction models require hours of compute per ensemble member, making real-time adaptation of model parameters to incoming observations infeasible without fast emulators (Lam et al., 2023; Bi et al., 2023); reservoir simulation for underground gas storage similarly demands repeated runs for history matching of injection-withdrawal cycles. The structural similarity across these domains motivates the generalisation argument developed in Section 2.3.

### 2.2 Surrogate Models in Petroleum Engineering: Forward-Only Approaches and Their Limitations

The use of surrogate and proxy models to accelerate petroleum engineering workflows has grown considerably over the past decade. Two broad categories are distinguished in the literature. **Data-driven models** are trained directly on field measurements; representative examples include the hydraulic fracturing design model of Morozov et al. (2020), which uses real production data to build a digital database for forecast, and the multi-parameter drilling risk identification approach of Feng et al. (2025). While such models can capture field-specific patterns, their accuracy is inherently bounded by the quality and coverage of available field data, which in oilfield applications is frequently limited by measurement uncertainty, reporting inconsistencies, and sparse spatial sampling.

**Physics-based synthetic surrogates**, by contrast, are trained on datasets generated by running a validated mechanistic simulator across a systematically sampled parameter space. This approach, sometimes termed *smart proxy modelling* (Vida et al., 2019), decouples the surrogate's generalization capability from field data availability. Applications in petroleum engineering include surrogate models for steady-state fracture growth (Kalyuzhnyuk et al., 2019), well production forecasting for unconventional reservoirs (Wang et al., 2021), and well completion design optimisation under parametric uncertainty (Moradi et al., 2022). Among these, the closest in spirit to a coupled pipeline is the work of Merzoug and Rasouli (2023), who combine a surrogate with metaheuristic algorithms for offset well geometry optimisation in the Bakken formation. However, the optimisation target in that work is a design variable (well spacing and completion parameters), not the calibration of physical model parameters to match observed production data — a distinction that is central to the present paper.

In the specific context of hydraulic fracture flowback, the most recent and comprehensive review of the field (Mingkun et al., 2025) confirms that no machine-learning or surrogate-based model with broad applicability and a physically grounded basis currently exists for this process. All surrogate studies cited above share a common structural limitation: they treat the proxy model as a **forward-only** emulator, providing fast predictions given a set of inputs, but making no use of the surrogate to close the loop back to field data through inverse calibration. As a result, any discrepancy between the surrogate's predictions and actual observed well behaviour — arising from geological heterogeneity, measurement noise, or unmodelled phenomena — cannot be corrected within the existing frameworks.

### 2.3 Cross-Domain Analogies and Universality of the Proposed Pipeline

The forward–inverse pipeline proposed in this paper is not specific to the fracture flowback problem. Its applicability is determined by three structural conditions: (A) a field-validated physical model exists that can generate synthetic training data; (B) the input parameter space can be reduced to a tractable set of governing parameters, whether through dimensional analysis or domain-specific feature selection; and (C) real observational data are available to serve as the adaptation target for the inverse step. Wherever these conditions hold simultaneously, the pipeline architecture — physical model, dimensionless reduction, surrogate forward model, inverse calibration — can be deployed with domain-specific instantiation.

Two domains provide direct structural analogies. In **numerical weather prediction**, global physics-based models such as the ECMWF Integrated Forecast System constitute the mechanistic layer. Recent machine-learning weather models — GraphCast (Lam et al., 2023) and Pangu-Weather (Bi et al., 2023) — demonstrate that surrogate emulators trained on reanalysis data can match or exceed operational forecast accuracy for medium-range prediction at a fraction of the computational cost, satisfying Condition A. However, these models do not incorporate an explicit inverse adaptation step: model parameters are fixed after training, and no mechanism exists to adjust them toward agreement with incoming real-time observations within a prescribed confidence bound. This is precisely the limitation that Conditions B and C, and the inverse component of our pipeline, are designed to address.

In **underground gas storage**, the seasonal injection-withdrawal cycle requires repeated reservoir simulation to predict deliverability and pressure dynamics across a range of operating scenarios. The structural parallel to the flowback case is direct: a computationally expensive simulator, a need for fast sensitivity analysis, and field pressure and flow rate measurements that serve as the adaptation target. *[Gleb: add reference here if available from the ВНР domain.]*

In both cases, the cross-domain argument is structural rather than empirical: domain-specific validation of the pipeline beyond the fracture flowback case demonstrated here is a direction for future work. The present paper establishes the pipeline architecture and demonstrates its efficacy on a well-characterised petroleum engineering problem with field validation.
