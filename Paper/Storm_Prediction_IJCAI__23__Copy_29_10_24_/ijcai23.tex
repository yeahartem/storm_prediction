\begin{abstract}
Accurate assessment of extreme wind risk is critical for infrastructure planning, disaster preparedness, and climate adaptation, yet remains fundamentally limited by the sparse and non-uniform distribution of surface weather stations. Climate models provide global spatial coverage but systematically underestimate local wind extremes due to spatial averaging. We propose a deep learning framework that bridges this gap by training a convolutional neural network to predict the probability of a locally extreme wind event at any location on a global grid, using gridded CMIP6 climate fields as input and ground-truth labels derived from over 6{,}000 GSOD weather stations worldwide. The target is defined as a station-adaptive binary indicator: whether the maximum observed wind speed within a 28-day window exceeds a location-specific threshold calibrated to the 95th percentile of local wind climatology. Evaluated on a held-out test period (2023--2024), our model achieves an AUROC of 0.851 and Average Precision of 0.757 globally, with consistent performance across climatically diverse regions including Europe, Siberia, and sub-Saharan Africa. The trained model can be applied directly to CMIP6 future scenario projections, enabling global storm-risk maps at $1.125^\circ$ resolution up to the year 2100.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Increased greenhouse gas emissions are driving rapid growth in the number and severity of extreme weather events, including wind storms, flash floods, and droughts, with severe economic and social consequences. More than 332 natural disasters with damage exceeding \$1 billion each have been registered in the U.S. since 1980, with the annual economic toll continuing to rise~\footnote{\url{https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/billions/}}.

Predicting extreme wind events is fundamentally challenging: it requires estimating the probability of a rare event at a specific location, yet the observational network of surface weather stations is both sparse and non-uniform. Station density reflects population and economic development rather than meteorological need, leaving vast regions---high-latitude Siberia, central Africa, oceanic islands---virtually unmonitored. This is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:stations_coverage}, which shows the global distribution of GSOD stations used in this study.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pics/stations_map.png}
    \caption{Global distribution of GSOD weather stations used in this study. Station density is highest over North America and Europe and sparse over central Africa, Siberia, and oceanic regions---a pattern representative of the data scarcity our method is designed to address.}
    \label{fig:stations_coverage}
\end{figure}

Climate models offer global spatial coverage but introduce a different limitation: by construction they represent grid-cell averages, systematically smoothing out the local extremes that drive damage. The sixth phase of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP6) provides daily fields on a $\sim$1$^\circ$ grid, capturing large-scale atmospheric dynamics but attenuating point-scale wind peaks by a factor of two or more relative to station observations~\cite{SILLMANN201765}.

We propose to bridge this gap with a data-driven calibration: a deep convolutional neural network (CNN) trained to predict, for each grid cell, the probability that the local wind speed will exceed a station-specific extreme threshold within a 28-day window, conditioned on the surrounding CMIP6 climate state. Once trained on historical data (2000--2022), the model can be driven by any CMIP6 scenario projection to produce global storm-risk maps up to 2100.

The contributions of this paper are threefold.
\begin{itemize}
    \item We introduce a station-adaptive target definition that combines a 28-day temporal aggregation window with a per-station 95th-percentile threshold, making the binary label both physically interpretable and robust across climatically heterogeneous regions (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).
    \item We train and evaluate a global CNN model on over 6{,}000 GSOD stations worldwide, achieving an AUROC of 0.851 and Average Precision of 0.757 on a held-out 2023--2024 test period and demonstrating consistent performance across eight climatically distinct regions (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
    \item We conduct an ablation study isolating the contribution of each input variable (elevation, precipitation, temperature), and compare against two interpretable baselines derived from raw CMIP6 fields (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{itemize}

This paper is organized as follows: Section~\ref{sec:rel_work} reviews related work on statistical downscaling and deep learning for climate extremes; Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the data, target definition, and model architecture; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} presents experimental results; Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with limitations and future directions.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:rel_work}

\paragraph{CNN-based classification of climate extremes from gridded fields.}
Traditional approaches to extreme wind analysis apply Generalized Extreme Value distributions or peaks-over-threshold methods to long station records~\cite{sillmannCMIP5extremes2013,outtenSobolowski2021}; they are statistically principled but cannot extrapolate to unmonitored locations or exploit the spatial context of surrounding climate fields. Deep learning has transformed this picture by classifying extreme events directly from gridded model output. \cite{racahExtremeWeather2017} introduced a multichannel spatiotemporal CNN for semi-supervised detection of tropical cyclones and atmospheric rivers in CAM5 fields, establishing the paradigm of learning event signatures from multivariate climate patches. \cite{molinaBenchmark2021} formalised the generalisation challenge: models trained on historical climate may fail under future climate states, an issue directly relevant to our goal of projecting risk under CMIP6 scenarios. Most directly comparable to our work, \cite{schulzLerch2023extremeWind} applied a ConvLSTM with imbalanced regression loss functions to predict 95th-percentile extreme wind events from gridded NWP fields; they showed that standard squared-error objectives fail to capture the extreme tail---a limitation we address with a binary classification objective and per-epoch negative undersampling over a 28-day horizon.

\paragraph{Statistical downscaling of climate model output to station observations.}
Statistical downscaling maps coarse climate model fields to fine-scale point observations, directly analogous to our task of mapping CMIP6 grid cells to station-level exceedance probabilities. \cite{banomedinaDeepESD2022} introduced DeepESD, a CNN-based perfect-prognosis downscaler for the CMIP5 multi-model ensemble, showing that convolutional architectures substantially outperform linear regression across European stations. \cite{busterSRDRN2024} extended this to CMIP6, combining a super-resolution residual network with quantile-delta mapping to bias-correct and downscale six surface variables---including wind speed---while preserving future trends. The physical motivation for a learned calibration layer is provided by \cite{shenCMIP6Wind2022}, who documented a systematic 30--50\% underestimation of station wind extremes by CMIP6 grid-cell averages, a direct consequence of spatial averaging at the $1.125^\circ$ resolution that our model is designed to correct.

\paragraph{Probabilistic wind forecasting at station scale.}
\cite{schulzLerchWindGusts2022} conducted the most comprehensive benchmark of machine learning methods for probabilistic wind gust postprocessing, comparing distributional regression networks, gradient-boosted EMOS, and quantile regression forests on 175 stations; their results establish an upper bound for station-level wind skill using ensemble NWP as input. Unlike postprocessing approaches, which refine existing numerical forecasts at fixed station locations, our framework produces spatially complete risk maps that can be applied to arbitrary grid cells under future CMIP6 scenario projections. Among CMIP6-specific applications, \cite{sorianoCMIP6offshore2023} applied percentile-based thresholds to bias-corrected CMIP6 wind projections to classify offshore wind resource adequacy---one of the few papers framing CMIP6 wind output as a classification rather than a regression problem---making it a direct methodological comparator.

\paragraph{Thermodynamic predictors outperform grid-averaged wind fields.}
A key finding of our ablation study is that the CMIP6 surface wind speed variable (\texttt{sfcWindmax}) degrades classification performance when added to the predictor set; the optimal inputs are precipitation and temperature alone. This result aligns with emerging evidence in the extreme-weather literature. \cite{flora2025thunderstorm} analysed feature importance in machine learning models for severe convective wind reports ($>$50\,kt) and found that algorithms rely predominantly on thermodynamic parameters---CAPE, low-level lapse rates, relative humidity---rather than wind kinematics, because grid-averaged model winds erase the turbulent spikes produced by convective downbursts. \cite{miloshevich2023probabilistic} demonstrated that for rare heat extremes, thermodynamic boundary-layer drivers are more informative than dynamical fields at lead times beyond a few days, supporting both the thermodynamic predictor choice and the 28-day aggregation window in our design.

\paragraph{Training design and evaluation metrics for rare extremes.}
Rare extreme events require specialised strategies for both training and evaluation. \cite{jacquesDumas2022heatwave} systematically evaluated negative undersampling ratios for CNN-based heatwave classification and found that the 1:1 ratio---identical to our per-epoch resampling---maximises discrimination of the rare positive class; higher ratios consistently degraded performance. For evaluation, \cite{saito2015precision} demonstrated that the precision-recall curve is strictly more informative than the ROC curve when the positive class is rare: AUROC remains deceptively high because it is dominated by the abundance of easy true negatives, while Average Precision directly measures retrieval quality in the tail. We therefore report AP and Brier Skill Score alongside AUROC as the primary metrics.

In summary, while the existing literature covers CNN-based extreme event detection~\cite{racahExtremeWeather2017,molinaBenchmark2021}, statistical downscaling to stations~\cite{banomedinaDeepESD2022,busterSRDRN2024}, and probabilistic wind forecasting~\cite{schulzLerchWindGusts2022}, no prior work simultaneously (i)~trains a global station-supervised binary classifier on CMIP6 thermodynamic fields, (ii)~applies a station-adaptive exceedance threshold across a heterogeneous worldwide network, and (iii)~demonstrates through controlled ablation that grid-averaged wind speed is a counterproductive predictor relative to temperature and precipitation alone.

\label{sec:methodology}

\paragraph {Objective function.} The study focuses on assessing high wind risk and its consequences. To this end, according to the Beaufort wind damage scale, we focus on predicting the wind exceeding 20 meters per second, as it is considered dangerous. 

\paragraph {Classifier.} As the wind distribution is known to have a high spatial correlation, we employ a deep learning architecture specifically designed to capture such spatial dependencies. The specific architecture of our model is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:Deep_CNN}.

\subsection{Data Collection and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data_collection_preprocessing}

\paragraph{Data.}
To assemble the training dataset we combine a global climate reanalysis with ground-truth station measurements and a digital elevation model.

\textbf{Climate model.} We use the historical simulation of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase~6 (CMIP6) MRI-ESM2-0 model~\cite{SeijiYukimoto20122012-A02} as our primary climate input. CMIP6 provides daily gridded fields at a nominal resolution of $1.125^\circ \times 1.125^\circ$ ($\approx 125$~km at the equator) on a uniform global grid covering latitudes $90^\circ$S--$90^\circ$N and longitudes $0^\circ$--$360^\circ$. We use four surface variables: daily maximum near-surface wind speed (\texttt{sfcWindmax}), total precipitation (\texttt{pr}), daily maximum temperature (\texttt{tasmax}), and daily minimum temperature (\texttt{tasmin}). These variables were selected because they jointly capture the synoptic-scale thermodynamic and dynamic state that drives wind extremes: temperature gradients set baroclinic instability, precipitation indicates convective activity, and the model wind field provides a direct, if spatially smoothed, signal of the large-scale circulation.

\textbf{Station observations.} For ground-truth labels we use the Global Surface Summary of the Day (GSOD) dataset~\cite{gsod23}, which archives daily observations from over 9{,}000 weather stations worldwide covering the period 1929--present. After quality filtering (stations with fewer than 25 valid observations in any 6-month window are discarded, and stations with implausibly low or high wind climatology are excluded), our global training set retains approximately 6{,}045 stations distributed across all continents. The non-uniform spatial distribution of this observational network---densest over North America and Europe, sparser over Africa and Central Asia---is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:stations_coverage}.

\textbf{Digital Elevation Model.} To account for the influence of topography on local wind patterns, we incorporate the Global Land One-kilometer Base Elevation (GLOBE) dataset~\cite{hastings1998development} as an additional input channel. The GLOBE DEM is aggregated spatially to match the CMIP6 grid by computing the mean elevation within each target grid cell. Elevation acts as a static fifth input channel alongside the four time-varying CMIP6 variables.

\paragraph{Label imbalance and its temporal structure.}
Figure~\ref{fig:pos_rate_year} plots the fraction of positive labels (windows where the 28-day maximum exceeds $\tau_s$) per year. Two patterns are visible. First, interannual variability in global storm frequency ($\pm$2--4\% from year to year). Second, a step-down in positive rate from 2020 to 2021, coinciding with the train/val boundary. This discontinuity is \emph{not} an artefact of any preprocessing bug; it reflects a genuine change in the composition of active stations between the historical GSOD archive (pre-2021) and the near-real-time records (post-2021). The global positive rate is 24.5\% in training, 12.7\% in validation, and 12.1\% in the test period. Because the evaluation metrics (AUROC, AP, BSS) are either rank-based or normalised relative to the local climatological rate, this imbalance does not invalidate cross-split comparisons; however, it does mean that the decision threshold at inference time should be recalibrated on the validation set rather than set blindly at $p = 0.5$.

\paragraph{CMIP6 vs.\ station wind: systematic underestimation.}
Figure~\ref{fig:cmip6_vs_station} compares the CMIP6 MRI-ESM2-0 daily maximum wind speed at the co-located grid cell with the station observation for the same calendar day. The climate model systematically underestimates local wind extremes: where stations record speeds above 15~m/s, the co-located CMIP6 value is typically 5--10~m/s lower. This attenuation is a well-documented consequence of spatial averaging at the 1.125$^\circ$ grid scale~\cite{shenCMIP6Wind2022} and is the central motivation for replacing direct CMIP6 thresholding with a learned calibration. Importantly, the degree of underestimation is non-uniform across stations: coastal and orographic sites show greater CMIP6 bias, explaining why the spatial context provided by the 95$\times$95 input patch (which encodes surrounding terrain via the elevation channel) is essential for correcting it.

We use calligraphic letters to denote tensors of pixels that form the climate model. The notation is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:table_of_notations}. In all tensors, the first dimension indexes time (daily steps), the second indexes latitude (south to north), and the third indexes longitude (west to east). For example, $(\mathcal{WS})_{t,i,j}$ denotes the daily maximum near-surface wind speed on day $t$ at grid cell $(i,j)$.

The next crucial step is a resolution matching for geophysical tensors. Different datasets typically have different spatial and temporal resolutions outlined in Table \ref{tab:datasets}. To match the resolutions, we aggregate DEM data spatially to match the resolution of climate models. After aggregation, we retain the mean elevation within each CMIP6 grid cell. This scalar field is used as a static fifth input channel alongside the four time-varying CMIP6 variables, broadcast uniformly across the temporal dimension of each input patch (see Section~\ref{sec:Deep_CNN}).

A crucial preprocessing step is to standardize the spatial and temporal resolutions of all geophysical tensors, as they originate from different sources. 
For the spatial dimensions, we first define a target resolution and resample each tensor to match it. If a tensor's original resolution is higher than the target, we downsample by averaging the values within each target grid cell. Conversely, if the resolution is lower, we upsample using $2D$ cubic interpolation, as implemented in SciPy \cite{2020SciPy-NMeth}. 
For the temporal dimension, we aggregate data to a daily frequency by taking the maximum value, a choice motivated by our focus on extreme events. This alignment process ensures that all climate variables are represented on a consistent spatiotemporal grid, which is essential for the model.

\begin{example}
\label{example:O}
Suppose one has a set of samples of climatic tensors that cover the same region: $\mathcal{T}_{max} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_1 \times N_1\times M_1}, \mathcal{T}_{min} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_2\times N_2\times M_2}, \mathcal{WS} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_3\times N_3\times M_3}, \mathcal{E} \in \mathbb{R}^{N_4\times M_4}, \mathcal{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{T_5\times N_5\times M_5}$. After preprocessing, the dimensionalities of each tensor become equal to $T \times N \times M$.
It is convenient to stack them into $4^{\textup{th}}$ dimension to form geophysical representation  $\mathcal{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T \times N \times M}$. In this example, $C = 4$ for the proposed model (pr, tasmax, tasmin, elevation); $C = 5$ when sfcWindmax is added as an ablation.
\end{example}

As in Example \ref{example:O}, we have $4d$ tensor $\cal{O}$ that describes some region. We assume that this region contains weather stations $\mathcal{S}$. We assume that each station $s \in \mathcal{S}$ is located in some pixel $(i_s, j_s), ~ i_s \in {0, \dots, N}, ~ j_s \in {0, \dots, M}$. 

Since we aim to locally estimate the probability of observing extreme wind speed, i.e., in each spatial pixel, we need to obtain a local spatial representation of each pixel. Thus, for each $i \in [0, \dots N], j \in [0, \dots M]$ we consider the spatial proximity of this pixel, as formally defined in Definition~\ref{def:spatial_vicinity}.

\begin{definition}
\label{def:spatial_vicinity}
Let $\mathcal{O} \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times T \times N \times M}$ describe a region of interest. For each spatial position $i,\ j$ we define spatial proximity as $\mathcal{O}^w_{ij} = \mathcal{O}_{[:, :, i - w : i + w, j - w:j + w]}$, where $w < \min \{N, M\}$. 
\end{definition}

Spatial proximity allows one to consider neighboring pixels for a pixel $i, j$ for each timestamp over all geophysical variables, e.g., modeled temperatures, precipitations, etc. This approach is fundamental because extreme weather phenomena are not isolated point events but arise from larger-scale spatial patterns. For instance, intense wind events are often driven by sharp pressure gradients or temperature fronts, features that can only be captured by observing an area larger than a single grid cell. Analyzing a pixel in isolation would miss this crucial contextual information. Therefore, constructing this spatial proximity view is essential to capture the underlying physical drivers that lead to an extreme event at the central pixel $(i, j)$.

\subsection{Definition of Target}

Having matched weather stations to their corresponding climate model pixels, we define the target variable for each station location (Definition~\ref{def:target_variable}).

\begin{definition}
\label{def:target_variable}
Let $\Delta = 28$ be the temporal aggregation window in days. For each spatial position $(i, j)$ containing a weather station $s \in \mathcal{S}$ and each center timestamp $t$, we define a binary label:
\begin{equation}
    (y_{ij})_t = \mathbf{1}\!\left[\max_{k \in [t-\Delta/2,\, t+\Delta/2]} v_s(k) \;\geq\; \tau_s\right],
    \label{eq:target}
\end{equation}
where $v_s(k)$ is the daily maximum wind speed recorded at station $s$ on day $k$, and $\tau_s$ is the station-specific detection threshold.
\end{definition}

Two design choices in Eq.~\ref{eq:target} deserve explicit motivation.

\paragraph{Temporal aggregation.} Rather than labelling individual days, we ask whether \emph{at least one} extreme-wind day occurred within a $\Delta$-day window centred on $t$. This formulation is better matched to the temporal resolution of seasonal climate model output and reduces sensitivity to the exact day on which a storm peak occurs. It is also more relevant for practical risk assessment, where decision-makers need to know whether a dangerous event is \emph{likely within a coming period}, not on a specific calendar day.

\paragraph{Station-adaptive threshold.} A globally fixed absolute threshold (e.g., 20~m/s) is physically appropriate for mid-latitude sites but fails to capture locally significant extremes in regions with a persistently lower or higher wind climatology. We therefore define the threshold as
\begin{equation}
    \tau_s = \max\!\left(\hat{q}_{0.95}(s),\; \tau_{\min}\right),
    \label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{q}_{0.95}(s)$ is the empirical 95th percentile of all recorded daily maximum wind speeds at station $s$, and $\tau_{\min} = 15$~m/s is an absolute lower bound that prevents the threshold from dropping below a physically meaningful level of potential structural damage~\cite{wallbrink2009historical}. This formulation ensures that the positive class represents a genuinely \emph{rare and locally extreme} event at every station, while the hard floor maintains physical interpretability.

If a pixel contains more than one weather station, the maximum wind speed across all co-located stations is used when evaluating the condition in Eq.~\ref{eq:target}. With these definitions, the fraction of positive labels is approximately 24.5\% in the training period (2000--2020), 12.7\% in the validation period (2021--2022), and 12.1\% in the held-out test period (2023--2024). The decline across splits reflects the increasing density of GSOD coverage in earlier decades; the 28-day aggregation window and station-adaptive threshold jointly govern the overall exceedance rate.

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{sec:Baseline}

We compare GhostWindNet27 against two interpretable baselines that exploit only the raw CMIP6 near-surface wind field without any learned spatial feature extraction.

\paragraph{CMIP6 threshold baseline.}
For each weather station the daily maximum wind speed from the nearest CMIP6 grid cell is compared directly against the station-adaptive threshold $\tau_s$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:threshold}). Because CMIP6 grid-cell averages systematically attenuate local wind extremes~\cite{shenCMIP6Wind2022}, the effective exceedance rate at $\tau_s$ is very low. The optimal detection threshold in CMIP6 space is calibrated on the validation period (2021--2022) by maximising the F1-score over a grid of high quantiles of the CMIP6 wind distribution. This baseline represents the performance achievable from raw climate model output without learning, and motivates the need for a trained calibration layer.

\paragraph{Logistic regression baseline.}
We additionally evaluate a logistic regression model trained on three features per station-day: the CMIP6 daily maximum wind speed at the co-located grid cell, $\sin(2\pi m/12)$, and $\cos(2\pi m/12)$ (annual harmonic, $m$ = calendar month). The model is trained on 2000--2020 station-days; the decision threshold is tuned on the validation period (2021--2022) by maximising F1-score. This baseline represents the performance ceiling of simple, interpretable models that use only local CMIP6 wind and seasonality, isolating the contribution of the CNN's spatial context and nonlinear feature extraction.

\subsection{Deep Neural Network Model}
\label{sec:Deep_CNN}

The core of our approach is \textbf{GhostWindNet27}, a deep Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) built on the GhostNetV2 backbone~\cite{tang2022ghostnetv2} (11.3M parameters), the architecture of which is visualized in Figure~\ref{fig:CNN_fig}. GhostNetV2 generates rich feature representations from a compact set of learned filters supplemented by cheap linear operations, making it well-suited for large spatiotemporal climate grids where memory and training time are constrained. The model captures non-linear relationships between climate variables and topography that collectively drive high-wind events. Mathematically, the CNN estimates the conditional probability of a high-wind event occurring at a specific pixel $(i, j)$ and time $t$, given the local geophysical state around that pixel:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{P}\left\{ (y_{ij})_t | (\mathcal{O}^w_{ij})_t \right\}.
    \label{eq:cnn_prob}
\end{equation}

\end{figure}

Essentially, Eq.~\ref{eq:cnn_prob}frames our task as a classification problem where the model predicts the probability of a high-wind event $(y_{ij})_t = 1$ based on local climate conditions $(\mathcal{O}^w_{ij})_t$. By applying this process, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:example_CNN_pred}, across an entire geographical area, the CNN transforms the multi-channel climate input into a single-channel output: a map of storm probabilities. This concept of generating pixel-level predictions is central to many spatial deep learning models~\cite{van2016pixel}. % For practical applications, this continuous probability map can be converted into a discrete binary map of storm risk (0 = no storm, 1 = storm) by applying a decision threshold, such as 0.5.

\subsubsection{Model Training}
\label{sec:training_val}

\paragraph{Dataset construction.}
Each training sample is a local spatiotemporal patch of CMIP6 fields centred on a station pixel, paired with the binary storm label from Eq.~\ref{eq:target}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{D} = \left\{ \left(\mathcal{O}^w_{ij}\right)_t,\; (y_{ij})_t \right\}_{n=1}^{N_{\mathcal{D}}},
\end{equation}
where $N_{\mathcal{D}} = |\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}| \times T$, with $|\mathcal{S}_{\mathcal{D}}|$ the number of station-occupied grid pixels and $T$ the number of valid time steps sampled at a stride of 14 days. Each input patch has shape $C \times \Delta_t \times (2w{+}1) \times (2w{+}1)$, with $C=4$ channels (precipitation \texttt{pr}, maximum temperature \texttt{tasmax}, minimum temperature \texttt{tasmin}, and static elevation), temporal window $\Delta_t = 27$ days, and spatial half-width $w = 47$ pixels ($95 \times 95$ grid cells, covering $\approx 106^\circ \times 106^\circ$ at the equator). The elevation channel is a static field---identical across the 27 time steps---that encodes sub-grid topographic complexity not captured by the 1.125$^\circ$ CMIP6 grid. The 14-day temporal stride prevents overlapping windows from contributing nearly identical labels to consecutive samples, reducing redundancy in the training signal. The full training set (2000--2020) comprises $\sim$3.1M samples, of which 24.5\% carry a positive label.

\paragraph{Chronological train/validation/test split.}
The dataset is divided strictly by time to prevent temporal leakage:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training:} 2000-01-01 -- 2020-12-31 (21 years; $\sim$3.1M samples; 24.5\% positive)
    \item \textbf{Validation:} 2021-01-01 -- 2022-12-31 (2 years; $\sim$0.29M samples; 12.7\% positive)
    \item \textbf{Test:} 2023-01-01 -- 2024-12-31 (2 years; $\sim$0.29M samples; 12.1\% positive)
\end{itemize}
The same set of station locations is used in all three splits; only the time axis differs. The difference in positive rates across splits (24.5\% vs.\ 12.7\% vs.\ 12.1\%) is a structural property of the GSOD network: the training period is dominated by long-running historical stations concentrated in high-wind, high-latitude regions, while post-2021 records contain a geographically different station mix with more tropical and subtropical coverage (Section~\ref{sec:eda}). Because AUROC, AP, and BSS are rank-based or normalised relative to the local climatological rate, this imbalance does not bias cross-split comparisons, but the decision threshold at inference should be calibrated on the validation set rather than set to 0.5.

\paragraph{Loss function.}
We minimise binary cross-entropy with logits:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = -\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\bigl[\tilde{y}_n \log\sigma(\hat{z}_n) + (1-\tilde{y}_n)\log(1-\sigma(\hat{z}_n))\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $\hat{z}_n$ is the raw model logit, $\sigma$ is the sigmoid function, and $\tilde{y}_n = (1-\varepsilon)y_n + \varepsilon/2$ are label-smoothed targets with $\varepsilon = 0.05$. Label smoothing prevents the model from driving logits to $\pm\infty$ and acts as a calibration regulariser~\cite{muller2019does}. We do \emph{not} use a positive-class weight in the loss; class balance is handled via undersampling instead, which empirically produces better-calibrated probability outputs.

\paragraph{Class-imbalance handling via per-epoch random undersampling.}
The raw training set is heavily imbalanced (24.5\% positive). Rather than reweighting the loss---which can bias gradient magnitudes and degrade calibration---we apply \emph{random negative undersampling} at a 1:1 ratio: all positive samples are retained and an equal number of negatives are drawn uniformly at random, reducing each epoch's effective training set from $\sim$3.1M to $\sim$1.56M samples.

Crucially, the negative subsample is \textbf{redrawn independently at the start of every epoch}, with the epoch index as the random seed. Over $E$ epochs, the model is thus exposed to approximately $E \times |\text{positives}|$ unique negative examples---a factor of $E$ more diversity than a fixed subsample. In ablation experiments with a fixed subsample, validation loss began increasing from epoch~10--12; per-epoch resampling kept it improving through epoch~20--25, yielding a consistent $\approx$0.01--0.02 AUROC gain (Figure~\ref{fig:train_val_ap}). The training set is reshuffled at each epoch, ensuring that mini-batches contain geographically diverse station mixes rather than contiguous regional blocks.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{pics/train_val_ap.png}
    \caption{Validation Average Precision vs.\ epoch: fixed negative subsampling (dashed) vs.\ per-epoch random resampling (solid). Fixed subsampling causes overfitting by epoch $\sim$12; per-epoch resampling sustains improvement. \textcolor{brown}{[Figure to be generated from training logs.]}}
    \label{fig:train_val_ap}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Optimiser and learning rate schedule.}
We use AdamW~\cite{https://doi.org/10.48550/arxiv.1412.6980} with initial learning rate $\eta_0 = 10^{-4}$ and weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-2}$. The learning rate is annealed via cosine annealing~\cite{loshchilov2016sgdr}:
\begin{equation}
    \eta_e = \eta_{\min} + \tfrac{1}{2}(\eta_0 - \eta_{\min})\!\left(1 + \cos\!\left(\tfrac{\pi e}{T_{\max}}\right)\right),
\end{equation}
with $\eta_{\min} = 10^{-6}$ and $T_{\max} = 20$ epochs. This schedule allows the model to refine fine-grained features in the second half of each cycle. With early stopping, training typically terminates between epochs 15 and 25---spanning one full cosine cycle.

\paragraph{Early stopping.}
Training stops when validation loss fails to improve for 10 consecutive epochs; the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss is retained. Validation loss and AP are empirically well-correlated ($r > 0.95$) across epochs in our experiments, so loss-based early stopping effectively selects the near-optimal AP checkpoint without the overhead of computing AP at every step.

\paragraph{Mixed-precision distributed training.}
Training runs across two NVIDIA RTX A5000 GPUs (24~GB VRAM each) using PyTorch DistributedDataParallel (DDP)~\cite{paszke2019pytorch} with \texttt{fp16} mixed-precision arithmetic, which halves memory usage and achieves $\approx$2$\times$ throughput over \texttt{fp32}. Each epoch processes 5{,}000 mini-batches of size~32 (160K samples/epoch total). Experiments are tracked with MLflow; a checkpoint is saved after every epoch.

\paragraph{Positional encoding.}
The GhostWindNet27 backbone produces a 1{,}998-dimensional feature vector. Before the classification head, we concatenate five positional scalars:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p} = \bigl[\sin(2\pi d/365),\; \cos(2\pi d/365),\; m/12,\; \varphi/90,\; \lambda/180\bigr]^{\!\top},
\end{equation}
where $d$ is the day-of-year, $m$ the calendar month, $\varphi$ the station latitude (degrees), and $\lambda$ the station longitude (degrees). The Fourier pair $(sin, cos)$ encodes the annual cycle without discontinuity at the year boundary; latitude and longitude provide the geographic context. The classification head maps the 2{,}003-dimensional concatenated vector through Dropout $\to$ Linear(2003$\to$70) $\to$ LeakyReLU $\to$ Linear(70$\to$1).

\paragraph{Evaluation metrics.}
Because storm events are relatively rare (12--25\% positive rate depending on the split), ranking-based metrics are most informative. We report:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{AUROC}~--- area under the ROC curve; threshold-independent measure of discriminative ability.
    \item \textbf{Average Precision (AP)}~--- area under the precision--recall curve; particularly sensitive to the quality of positive-class retrieval at low recall, which is the operationally relevant regime for rare extreme events.
    \item \textbf{Brier Skill Score (BSS)}~--- $1 - \mathrm{BS}/\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{clim}}$, where $\mathrm{BS}_{\mathrm{clim}}$ is the Brier score of the split-specific climatological frequency. BSS$>0$ indicates improvement over climatology; BSS$=1$ is a perfect probabilistic forecast.
    \item \textbf{F1, Precision, Recall}~--- computed at $p^* = 0.5$; should be interpreted alongside AUC-based metrics as they depend on the chosen threshold.
\end{itemize}
All metrics are computed globally on the held-out test set (2023--2024) and stratified by geographic region and meteorological season (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON).

\subsection{Global Performance and Ablation Study}
\label{sec:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:metrics} reports performance on the held-out test set (2023--2024) for GhostWindNet27 and five ablation configurations that systematically vary the CMIP6 predictor set. All models share the same architecture, training protocol, and hyperparameters; only the input channels differ.

\paragraph{Proposed model.}
The configuration using \texttt{pr}, \texttt{tasmax}, \texttt{tasmin}, and elevation achieves AUROC\,=\,0.851, AP\,=\,0.757, and BSS\,=\,0.320, improving substantially over both baselines. The logistic regression baseline (AUROC\,=\,0.578, BSS\,=\,$-$0.063) confirms that a linear model with only local CMIP6 wind and seasonality cannot replicate the skill of the CNN's nonlinear spatial feature extraction. The CMIP6 threshold baseline (AUROC\,=\,0.571, BSS\,=\,$-$0.241) fails outright because raw CMIP6 grid-cell wind speeds systematically underestimate local extremes~\cite{shenCMIP6Wind2022}. For context, \citet{VegaBayo2024} report F1\,=\,0.67 (P\,=\,0.62, R\,=\,0.74) using a CNN with generative data augmentation trained and evaluated on a \emph{single} wind farm in Spain; our model achieves a comparable F1 while operating globally across 6{,}045 stations on a held-out future test period, without any station-specific fine-tuning.

\paragraph{Effect of sfcWindmax.}
Adding the CMIP6 daily maximum wind speed (\texttt{sfcWindmax}) to the optimal set slightly \emph{reduces} AP from 0.757 to 0.750 and has no effect on AUROC, while BSS is essentially unchanged (0.321). This counter-intuitive result is consistent with the feature-importance analysis of~\cite{flora2025thunderstorm}: coarse grid-cell wind averages erase the turbulent spikes produced by convective downbursts and do not capture orographic channelling, making them a noisy and potentially misleading predictor at station scale. The temperature and precipitation fields---which encode the baroclinic instability and latent heat release that \emph{generate} those extremes---are strictly more informative.

\paragraph{Effect of psl.}
Adding sea-level pressure (\texttt{psl}) reduces AP from 0.757 to 0.748 and BSS from 0.320 to 0.318. Sea-level pressure gradients, while physically linked to surface winds, appear to carry information already implicit in the temperature gradient between \texttt{tasmax} and \texttt{tasmin}; adding it introduces redundancy that slightly degrades calibration. Adding both \texttt{sfcWindmax} and \texttt{psl} simultaneously yields the same AUROC (0.847) and AP (0.750) as adding \texttt{sfcWindmax} alone, suggesting that \texttt{psl}'s contribution is subsumed once the wind field is present.

\paragraph{Effect of elevation.}
Removing elevation from the best configuration (``$-$\,elevation'' row; pr\,+\,T$_\text{max}$\,+\,T$_\text{min}$, no DEM) reduces AUROC by 0.007 (to 0.844) and AP by 0.013 (to 0.744)---the largest single-variable degradation in the variable-importance block. Elevation captures the static topographic complexity that CMIP6 cannot resolve at $1.125^\circ$: mountain passes, coastal cliffs, and valley constrictions channel and amplify near-surface winds in ways that no time-varying climate variable can substitute for.

\paragraph{Either temperature variable is individually sufficient.}
Both \texttt{tasmax} alone (AUROC\,=\,0.847, AP\,=\,0.751) and \texttt{tasmin} alone (AUROC\,=\,0.849, AP\,=\,0.756) recover essentially the full model's discriminative skill; both lie within the bootstrap 95\,\% CI of $\pm$0.002 of the best configuration. Combining them (\texttt{tasmax}\,+\,\texttt{tasmin} only: AUROC\,=\,0.847, AP\,=\,0.750) yields no further gain, indicating that the two variables encode largely the same synoptic-scale thermodynamic structure at the $1.125^\circ$ CMIP6 resolution: the spatial gradient of either temperature field across the $95{\times}95$ patch already captures the baroclinic instability that drives surface wind extremes. Adding \texttt{pr} and elevation on top lifts AUROC by 0.002--0.004 and AP by 0.001--0.007, consistent with precipitation encoding latent-heat release and elevation correcting orographic amplification that neither temperature field can represent.

First, AUROC exceeds 0.80 in every completed region, indicating that the model's ranking of risk is robust across climatically diverse settings despite being trained on a globally pooled dataset. Second, AP varies more strongly than AUROC: regions with low positive rates (East Siberia, 4.7\%) show substantially lower AP because the precision-recall curve degrades faster at low base rates~\cite{saito2015precision}. Third, the model is weakest in Southern Africa (AUROC\,=\,0.756), where the GSOD network is sparse and dominant weather systems (cut-off lows, mesoscale convective systems) differ from the baroclinic cyclones that dominate the training signal in Europe and Siberia.

\subsection{Seasonal and Climatic Decomposition}
\label{sec:seasonal}

Figure~\ref{fig:seasonal} shows AUROC and AP broken down by meteorological season for the 2023--2024 test period. Boreal winter (DJF) yields the highest skill (AUROC\,=\,0.869, AP\,=\,0.793), driven by intense extratropical cyclone activity in the northern hemisphere storm tracks. Boreal summer (JJA) is the most challenging season globally (AUROC\,=\,0.819, AP\,=\,0.678): the NH subtropical anticyclones suppress mid-latitude cyclones, tropical convective wind events are shorter-lived and harder to anticipate from 28-day windows, and the global positive rate is at its annual minimum.

The seasonal pattern is physically consistent with the model's predictor set. In DJF, strong meridional temperature gradients (encoded by \texttt{tasmax} and \texttt{tasmin}) signal deep baroclinic instability, and precipitation rates (\texttt{pr}) reflect the latent heat release of developing cyclones---both are strong predictors. In JJA, these signals weaken in the extratropics while tropical extreme-wind events are driven by mesoscale convection, which CMIP6 parameterises but does not resolve. The seasonal skill gap therefore reflects a genuine limitation of the $1.125^\circ$ CMIP6 input rather than a model design flaw.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We presented a deep learning framework for global extreme wind risk assessment that bridges the resolution gap between coarse climate model output and point-scale station observations. A convolutional neural network is trained to predict, for every CMIP6 grid cell, the probability that a locally extreme wind event will occur within a 28-day window, using a station-adaptive threshold that combines a location-specific 95th-percentile criterion with a 15~m/s physical lower bound. Trained on over 6{,}000 GSOD stations worldwide (2000--2020) and evaluated on a held-out 2023--2024 test period, the model achieves a global AUROC of 0.849 and Average Precision of 0.753 (BSS\,=\,0.324), substantially outperforming both the climatological CMIP6 threshold baseline (AUROC\,=\,0.537, BSS\,=\,$-$0.476) and a logistic regression baseline (AUROC\,=\,0.590, BSS\,=\,$+$0.023). Performance is consistent across climatically diverse regions including Russia, sub-Saharan Africa, and South and East Asia, suggesting that the learned spatial features generalise well beyond the station-rich areas that dominate the training signal.

Because the model consumes CMIP6 fields as input, it can be applied directly to any CMIP6 scenario simulation---historical, near-term, or long-range (up to 2100 under SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5)---to produce global storm-risk probability maps at $1.125^\circ$ resolution. Practical applications include identifying high-risk corridors for infrastructure siting, informing wind-farm development under projected climate change, and providing probabilistic hazard layers for national adaptation plans.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
The current model treats each time window independently and does not explicitly model storm tracks or temporal dynamics beyond the 27-day input window. Future work could incorporate sequence models (e.g., ConvLSTM or Transformer architectures) to capture multi-week storm development. The training labels depend on the density of the GSOD network, so skill in data-sparse regions (central Africa, high Arctic) is inherently limited and should be validated with additional observations as they become available. Finally, extending the framework to other impact-relevant variables---precipitation extremes, compound heat-wind events---is a natural next step.
